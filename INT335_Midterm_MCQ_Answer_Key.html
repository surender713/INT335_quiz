Unit 1 — Foundations of Learning, Creativity & Design Thinking

Thinking Modes

Q1

Case: Meera, a third-year B.Tech student, spends three days on a circuit-design problem for her mini-project, trying every method she knows without success. A friend advises her to go for a long walk and stop thinking about it. The next morning, while brushing her teeth, a workable approach suddenly occurs to her.

Which explanation best fits what happened?

  1. A. Diffuse mode works best on an empty mind, so her three days of effort were unnecessary.
  2. B. Her focused mode kept running unchanged during the walk and simply finished the calculation.
  3. C. Her diffuse mode reorganised material she had already struggled with in focused mode and produced a connection. ✔
  4. D. The walk switched her learning style from Visual to Kinesthetic, which solved the problem.

Answer: C

The diffuse mode does not work on an empty mind; it reorganises material you have already struggled with. Rule: struggle first (focused), then step away (diffuse).

Q2

Case: Two students have ten days to solve an open design challenge. Arjun reads the brief once on Day 1 and then does nothing for nine days, saying his 'subconscious will work on it'. Neel works intensively for two days, then alternates between short breaks and further work.

Who is more likely to reach a creative breakthrough, and why?

  1. A. Neither, because creative breakthroughs depend only on learning style and not on thinking modes.
  2. B. Both equally, because switching between modes is random and unrelated to study habits.
  3. C. Neel, because diffuse processing needs material previously wrestled with in focused mode, and effective problem-solving alternates the two modes. ✔
  4. D. Arjun, because a longer rest gives the diffuse mode more time to build broad connections.

Answer: C

Arjun gave the diffuse mode nothing to reorganise. Neel struggled first and then stepped away, which is exactly the alternation the notes recommend.

Q3

Case: A team lead at a software firm must (i) trace a known bug line by line through an established debugging procedure, and (ii) later come up with an unconventional concept for a new feature.

Which thinking modes best suit tasks (i) and (ii)?

  1. A. (i) Diffuse mode; (ii) Focused mode
  2. B. Both diffuse, because both tasks involve problem-solving
  3. C. (i) Focused mode; (ii) Diffuse mode ✔
  4. D. Both focused, because engineering work is logical

Answer: C

Focused mode = concentrated attention using established pathways (procedures, code, analysis). Diffuse mode = relaxed, associative thinking that makes broader connections (brainstorming, innovative design).

Q4

Which statement about the diffuse mode is correct?

  1. A. It is the mode used for solving equations and writing code
  2. B. It follows established neural pathways to carry out procedures step by step
  3. C. It makes broader connections among ideas and is linked to brainstorming and innovative design ✔
  4. D. It works best on a problem you have never thought about before

Answer: C

Diffuse mode = relaxed, associative thinking that makes broader connections. Focused mode handles equations, code, data and procedures.

Q5

Case: A writer has spent hours drafting and is stuck on how to resolve her plot.

According to the course notes, what is the best next move?

  1. A. Keep forcing focused effort without any break
  2. B. Take a break such as a walk so diffuse processing can work, then return ✔
  3. C. Ask friends to judge every line immediately
  4. D. Delete the whole draft so the mind is empty

Answer: B

When stuck, take a break to allow diffuse processing. The most effective problem-solving alternates between focused and diffuse modes.

Q6 Theory · 5 marks

Differentiate focused and diffuse thinking modes. Explain why effective problem-solving alternates between them.

Model answer
Focused mode: concentrated attention on a specific task using established neural pathways; logical, structured, analytical, sequential. Examples: solving equations, writing code, following procedures.
Diffuse mode: relaxed thinking that makes broader connections among ideas; creative, exploratory, flexible, associative. Examples: brainstorming, creative writing, innovative design.
Alternation: when stuck, take a break (rest or a walk) to allow diffuse processing, then return to focus/study.
Condition: the diffuse mode does not work on an empty mind; it reorganises material already struggled with. Struggle first, then step away.
Q7 Theory · 5 marks

Case: A student starts a hard design assignment the night before the deadline and plans to 'sleep on it and let the subconscious solve it'.

Explain what is wrong with this plan using the focused/diffuse model, and advise a better schedule.

Model answer
Wrong: the diffuse mode needs raw material from earlier focused effort; with no prior struggle there is nothing to reorganise, and too little time for alternation.
Better schedule: start early; work in focused mode on the problem (struggle first); step away (walk, rest, sleep); return to focus; repeat over several days.
Track where and when your best ideas arrive (Activity 1.1) to plan breaks.

Learning Styles (VARK)

Q8

Case: A college announces that after a VARK questionnaire every student will be taught only in their preferred style, claiming this 'guarantees higher marks'. A Design Thinking student is asked to comment.

Which critique is most consistent with the course notes?

  1. A. The research is irrelevant because any idea that becomes popular must be evidence-backed.
  2. B. The claim is well supported by controlled studies, so the policy is correct.
  3. C. VARK is a useful prompt for self-reflection, but the claim that teaching in the preferred style improves results has repeatedly failed in controlled studies. ✔
  4. D. VARK is useless even for reflection, so students should avoid thinking about how they study.

Answer: C

Treat a VARK result as a nudge toward self-awareness, not a rule. The notes also stress that an idea can be hugely popular without research support, and that retrieval practice, spacing and interleaving are the strategies that do replicate.

Q9

Case: Using Activity 1.2 scoring (V = statements 1+5, A = 2+6, R = 3+7, K = 4+8), a student rates statements 1 to 8 as 2, 4, 5, 1, 3, 2, 4, 3 respectively.

Which VARK letter scores highest?

  1. A. R (Reading/Writing) ✔
  2. B. A (Auditory)
  3. C. V (Visual)
  4. D. K (Kinesthetic)

Answer: A

V = 2+3 = 5; A = 4+2 = 6; R = 5+4 = 9; K = 1+3 = 4. R is highest. (Remember this is a reflection prompt, not a rule for how you must study.)

Q10

In the VARK model, what does 'K' stand for and prefer?

  1. A. Kinesthetic: practical, hands-on experience ✔
  2. B. Key-learning: discussion and debate
  3. C. Kinetic: watching videos and animations
  4. D. Knowledge: textbooks and written notes

Answer: A

V = Visual, A = Auditory, R = Reading/Writing, K = Kinesthetic (practical, hands-on experience).

Q11 Theory · 5 marks

Explain the VARK model and critically evaluate the claim that teaching in a student's preferred style improves results.

Model answer
VARK: Visual (diagrams, charts, videos), Auditory (listening, discussion), Reading/Writing (books, notes), Kinesthetic (hands-on practice).
Evidence: VARK is a useful prompt for reflecting on how you study, but the stronger claim, that being taught in your preferred style improves results, has repeatedly failed to hold up in controlled studies.
Treat the result as a nudge toward self-awareness, not a rule.
Strategies that do replicate: retrieval practice, spacing and interleaving.
Design-thinking lesson: a popular idea may not be research-backed, and what people say they want is not always what works.

Functional Fixedness & Cognitive Blocks

Q12

Case: During a hackathon, a team needs a lightweight stand to hold a sensor above a table. Someone points at an empty cardboard tube from a kitchen roll and says, 'That's just rubbish.' Another member says, 'Hold on — it is hollow, light, rigid and cylindrical,' and uses it as the stand.

Which technique did the second member use to overcome functional fixedness?

  1. A. Rejecting ideas early so the team could converge faster
  2. B. Listing the object's properties and finding a use for each, rather than thinking of its usual purpose ✔
  3. C. Surveying users on how much they like cardboard tubes
  4. D. Following the team's conventional thinking in order to fit in

Answer: B

Functional fixedness is seeing objects only for their conventional purpose. The way out is to stop thinking about the object and start listing its properties, then find a use for each property.

Q13

Case: A senior engineer with twenty years' experience is given a new thermal problem. Without studying how it differs from earlier ones, he proposes the same heat-sink-based solution he used in his last six projects.

Which cognitive block is most clearly at work?

  1. A. Premature judgment
  2. B. Fear of failure
  3. C. Conformity
  4. D. The expertise trap ✔

Answer: D

The expertise trap is over-reliance on past solutions: the more you know, the more you reach for what worked before.

Q14

Case: In a class brainstorm, Sana has an unusual idea but stays silent because a classmate laughed at her suggestion last week. Meanwhile the other five students keep repeating variations of the first idea that was spoken.

Which cognitive blocks are most evident for Sana and for the others, respectively?

  1. A. Premature judgment; expertise trap
  2. B. Expertise trap; premature judgment
  3. C. Conformity; fear of failure
  4. D. Fear of failure (criticism); conformity ✔

Answer: D

Sana hesitates because she fears criticism (fear of failure). The others follow the conventional, already-spoken line of thought to fit in (conformity).

Q15

Functional fixedness is best defined as:

  1. A. Hesitation to propose ideas because of fear of criticism

  3. C. The tendency to see objects only for their traditional purpose, limiting creative problem-solving ✔
  4. D. Rejecting ideas before exploring them fully

Answer: C

The other options define fear of failure, premature judgment and the expertise trap respectively.

Q16

Case: A mechanic needs to seal a leaking pipe. A discarded cycle inner tube lies nearby, but he sees it only as 'a tube for tyres' and goes to buy a rubber seal.

Which concept explains his behaviour?

  2. B. Fear of failure

He sees the object only for its conventional purpose, although a cut piece of the tube would serve as a seal.

Q17

Case: During a design review, a manager rejects each suggestion within seconds, before the team has explored how it might work.

Which cognitive block does this show?

  1. A. Conformity
  2. B. Functional fixedness
  3. C. Premature judgment ✔
  4. D. The expertise trap

Answer: C

Premature judgment is rejecting ideas before exploring them fully. It is also why judgment must be deferred during ideation.

Q18

According to the notes, Design Thinking helps break cognitive blocks through:

  1. A. Empathy, experimentation, collaboration and structured creative problem-solving ✔
  2. B. Finishing each phase completely before starting the next
  3. C. Strict reuse of previously successful solutions
  4. D. Keeping users out of the process to avoid bias

Answer: A

DT links directly to the blocks: empathy challenges assumptions, experimentation reduces fear of failure, and collaboration widens perspectives.

Q19 Theory · 5 marks

Define functional fixedness. Describe the property-listing technique and demonstrate it on an everyday object of your own choice (list at least four properties with uses).

Model answer
Definition: tendency to see objects only for their traditional purpose, limiting creative problem-solving.
Technique: stop thinking about the object, list its properties, then find a use for each property.
Example, an old CD: reflective -> signal mirror, bird scarer; flat and round -> coaster, drawing template; has a central hole -> hang as a decoration, spindle/stand; thin and rigid -> scraper, spacer.
Benefit: properties unlock uses that thinking about 'a CD' never would.
Q20 Theory · 10 marks

Explain four common cognitive blocks with an original example of each, and state how Design Thinking helps overcome them.

Model answer
Fear of failure: hesitation to propose ideas due to fear of criticism (e.g., a student stays silent in a group).
Conformity: following conventional thinking to fit in (e.g., everyone repeats the first idea).
Premature judgment: rejecting ideas before exploring them (e.g., 'too costly' said immediately).
Expertise trap: over-reliance on past solutions (e.g., reusing last project's design).
DT remedies: empathy, experimentation (cheap prototypes make failure safe), collaboration ('yes, and'), and structured creative problem-solving that separates generating from judging.

Creativity, Invention & Innovation

Q21

Case: A university lab builds the first working prototype of a battery chemistry that has never existed before, but no company has adopted it. Separately, a start-up takes ordinary existing batteries and builds a battery-swap service for e-rickshaw drivers that thousands use daily.

How should the two be classified according to the course?

  1. A. Lab: innovation; start-up: invention
  2. B. Lab: creativity only; start-up: invention
  3. C. Both are inventions, since both involve batteries
  4. D. Lab: invention; start-up: innovation ✔

Answer: D

Invention = creating something entirely new. Innovation = successfully implementing ideas to create value. Creativity becomes innovation only when it delivers value.

Q22

Case: A local tiffin service adds a mobile app for ordering its existing meals. A new firm, with no restaurants, launches shared cloud kitchens that deliver office meals at half the price to people who previously could not afford regular ordering.

Which classification is most accurate?

  1. A. The app is a disruptive innovation; the cloud-kitchen firm is an incremental improvement
  2. B. The app is an incremental improvement; the cloud-kitchen firm is a disruptive innovation ✔
  3. C. Both are disruptive because both use technology
  4. D. Both are incremental because both sell meals

Answer: B

Incremental = small but meaningful improvements to an existing product or process. Disruptive = a new product, service or business model that transforms markets with simpler, cheaper or more accessible solutions and serves unmet needs.

  4. D. Creativity: implementing ideas for value; Invention: generating ideas; Innovation: creating something new

Answer: A

Creativity becomes innovation only when it delivers value.

How is this best described?

  2. B. Incremental invention ✔

Q25

Case: A firm launches a simple, pay-as-you-go solar lamp service for households without reliable electricity, creating a new market and challenging kerosene suppliers.

What makes this a disruptive innovation?

  1. A. It offers a simpler, cheaper, more accessible solution to unmet needs and creates a new market ✔

Answer: A

Disruptive innovation transforms markets with simpler, cheaper or more accessible solutions, challenges incumbents and focuses on unmet customer needs.

Q26 Theory · 5 marks

Differentiate creativity, invention and innovation with one original example each. Why does creativity become innovation only when it delivers value?

Model answer
Creativity: ability to generate original and useful ideas (e.g., imagining a bus that adjusts its route to demand).
Invention: creating something entirely new that did not exist before (e.g., the first working device of a new type).
Innovation: successfully implementing creative ideas to create value (e.g., a ride-pooling service that thousands use).
Idea -> creativity; new thing -> invention; value -> innovation. An idea or invention that no one uses creates no value and so is not yet innovation.
Q27 Theory · 5 marks

Distinguish incremental invention from disruptive innovation. Give two examples of each and list the characteristics of disruptive innovation.

Model answer
Incremental: small but meaningful improvements to an existing product/process (e.g., USB to USB Type-C; petrol cars to hybrid cars).
Disruptive: a new product, service or business model that transforms markets with simpler, cheaper or more accessible solutions (e.g., streaming replacing rental stores; ride-hailing replacing traditional taxi booking).
Characteristics: creates new markets, challenges incumbents, focuses on unmet customer needs.

Kinds of Problems

Q28

Case: Consider three tasks: (a) compute the maximum bending stress in a beam under given loads; (b) design a mobile app that improves student well-being on a campus; (c) reduce air pollution across a metropolitan region where industry, commuters, farmers and regulators have conflicting interests.

How should the problems be classified?

  1. A. (a) well-defined; (b) wicked; (c) ill-defined
  2. B. (a) well-defined; (b) ill-defined; (c) wicked ✔
  3. C. (a) ill-defined; (b) wicked; (c) well-defined
  4. D. (a) wicked; (b) ill-defined; (c) well-defined

Answer: B

Well-defined: clear goals and one correct answer. Ill-defined: unclear goals or paths, many solutions (app design). Wicked: complex and interconnected, answers are better or worse, not right or wrong (climate, poverty, healthcare).

Q29

Which is a feature of wicked problems?

  1. A. They are complex and interconnected, and solutions are better or worse rather than right or wrong ✔
  2. B. They have clear goals and constraints
  3. C. They have one correct answer
  4. D. They are solved by applying a standard formula

Answer: A

Examples: climate, poverty, healthcare.

Q30

Why does the course say Design Thinking exists?

  1. A. Engineering education is useless for all kinds of problems
  2. B. Ill-defined and wicked problems do not yield to the methods that work on well-defined ones ✔
  3. C. Well-defined problems cannot be solved by engineers
  4. D. Because linear pipelines are always faster

Answer: B

Traditional engineering education trains you superbly on well-defined problems; the other two kinds need iterative, human-centred methods.

Q31 Theory · 5 marks

Explain well-defined, ill-defined and wicked problems with an example of each, and state why traditional engineering education alone is not enough.

Model answer
Well-defined: clear goals, constraints and solution; one correct answer (equations, puzzles, technical fixes).
Ill-defined: unclear goals or paths, many possible solutions (app design, marketing strategy).
Wicked: complex and interconnected; solutions are better or worse, not right or wrong (climate, poverty, healthcare).
Traditional engineering education trains students superbly on well-defined problems; the other two kinds do not yield to the same methods, so human-centred, iterative approaches (Design Thinking) are needed.

Design Thinking: History & Frameworks

Q32

Case: Two groups are given the same unfamiliar layout puzzle. Group X spends most of its time analysing the rules before attempting any arrangement. Group Y quickly tries many arrangements, learns from what fails, and refines.

In line with Lawson's experiment, which description is correct?

  1. A. X behaves like designers (solution-focused); Y behaves like scientists (problem-focused)
  2. B. X behaves like scientists (problem-focused); Y behaves like designers (solution-focused) ✔
  3. C. Both are solution-focused because both aim to finish the puzzle
  4. D. X is solution-focused and Y is problem-focused because Y analysed its failures

Answer: B

Lawson's key insight: scientists analyse thoroughly before proposing solutions; designers create and test many solutions rapidly, learning through iteration.

Q33

Case: A start-up's smart lunchbox is loved by test users, and engineers confirm it can be manufactured. But the cost of making and shipping each unit is higher than the selling price, with no route to profit.

Which IDEO lens is failing, and what does that imply?

  1. A. Viability; meeting only two of the three lenses produces a concept that fails in practice ✔
  2. B. Desirability; users do not want the product
  3. C. Feasibility; the product cannot be built
  4. D. None; strong desirability alone guarantees innovation

Answer: A

IDEO's three overlapping lenses: desirability (do people want it?), feasibility (can we build it?), viability (is it economically sustainable?). Genuine innovation lies where all three overlap.

Q34

Case: A company builds an internal HR portal using Plan → Build → Test → Ship over fourteen months. In launch week, employees say the navigation does not match how they work, and fixing it requires redoing large parts of the build.

Which characteristic of this process most directly caused the costly rework?

  4. D. The team followed the Stanford d.school loop too strictly

Traditional waterfall is linear, each phase finished before the next, with user feedback at the end. Human-centred design is iterative, with empathy, feedback, prototyping and refinement throughout (CO1).

Q43 Theory · 5 marks

Trace the history and evolution of Design Thinking from the 1960s to today.

Model answer
Mid-1960s: design methods movement; Rittel coins 'wicked problems'.
1969–70s: Herbert Simon presents design as a way of thinking.
1982–87: Nigel Cross (Designerly Ways of Knowing); Rowe publishes Design Thinking.
1991: IDEO founded; pioneers human-centred design.
2005: Stanford d.school established.
Today: applied across industries worldwide.
Q44 Theory · 5 marks

Explain Lawson's experiment and what it implies for how engineering students should approach an open design brief.

Model answer
Key insight: scientists are problem-focused (analyse thoroughly before proposing solutions); designers are solution-focused (create and test many solutions rapidly, learning through iteration).
Implication: on open briefs, do not wait for complete analysis; prototype many options early, test, and learn from feedback; use analysis to interpret results rather than to delay action.
Q45 Theory · 10 marks

Describe the five stages of the Stanford d.school framework. Why is the process described as iterative rather than linear?

Model answer
1. Empathize: understand users' needs and challenges.
2. Define: pinpoint the core problem.
3. Ideate: generate many ideas.
4. Prototype: build a simple model.
5. Test: evaluate and refine with users.
Iterative: results from testing feed back into earlier stages (even Empathize); you loop back and improve rather than finishing each phase once.
Q46 Theory · 10 marks

Case: A company launches a premium smart helmet with advanced sensors, priced at ₹18,000, aimed at two-wheeler riders in small towns. Sales are very low.

Analyse the likely reasons using IDEO's desirability, feasibility and viability lenses, and state what evidence you would collect before relaunching.

Model answer
Desirability: do small-town riders want or trust it? Many may see no clear problem solved, or the price may be too high (price vs value).
Feasibility: technology works, but servicing, charging and distribution in small towns may be hard.
Viability: at this price, volume is too low for sustainable economics.
A product that sits outside the overlap of all three lenses fails in practice.
Evidence: interviews and observation of riders (what they do, not just say), willingness-to-pay tests, prototype trials with a simpler model, cost and service-network analysis.
Q47 Theory · 5 marks

Compare the traditional waterfall model with human-centred design, and explain why this distinction is central to CO1.

Model answer
Waterfall: linear, step-by-step (Plan, Build, Test, Ship); each phase is finished before the next; user feedback arrives at the end, so changes are costly.
HCD: iterative and user-focused; empathy, continuous feedback, prototyping and refinement throughout; testing loops back to empathy.
CO1 asks students to explain human-centred principles and differentiate iterative workflows from linear pipelines; early user feedback reduces the cost of failure.

Q49

Case: A start-up tests a paper mock-up with five users in the first week and discovers a fatal navigation flaw, instead of finding it after six months of coding.

Which DT belief does this illustrate?

Q73 Theory · 10 marks

Q74 Theory · 5 marks

Explain the What–How–Why framework using an example of your own, and state why inference must be labelled.

Model answer
WHAT: observe objectively what the user does (e.g., opens a ticket app, selects a train, scrolls fares).
HOW: analyse how they do it, including effort, emotions and difficulties (e.g., compares options repeatedly, hesitates at payment).
WHY: identify motivations, emotions and needs (e.g., worried about price or refund), the stage where inference begins.
Inference must be labelled and verified because observation without interpretation is data, and interpretation without observation is guesswork.

Stay anonymous: pseudonyms or initials.
Do not photograph people: sketch the scene.
Be safe: open, public, daylight, in pairs; avoid residential spaces; consult the mentor if in doubt.
Informed consent is itself a practice of human-centred design: it respects the user.

Q111 Theory · 5 marks

Differentiate divergent and convergent thinking. Why must they not be mixed, and what goes wrong at the extremes?
